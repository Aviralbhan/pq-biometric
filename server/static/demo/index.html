How it works

1. Capture

You look at the webcam and do two quick actions the system picks at random, like "blink twice" or "turn your head left".

A recording made earlier cannot know these actions, so this proves a real person is there right now.

→

2. Post-quantum channel

Your laptop puts a tamper-proof seal (a digital signature) on the video, then locks it in an envelope only the server can open.

Both use new post-quantum methods (ML-DSA and ML-KEM), so even a future quantum computer cannot fake the seal or open the envelope.

→

3. Verification server

First the server checks the envelope: is it new, unopened, meant for this exact payment, and really from your laptop?

Then AI checks the face: is it you, and is it live, not a photo, a blend of two faces, a deepfake or a fake camera? The final yes/no is sealed too, so nobody can change it later.

→

4. Evaluation

We run everything twice, once with today's cryptography and once with post-quantum cryptography, to measure how much extra time and data the new one costs.

We also test each AI check on thousands of public sample photos (see the Results tab).

Glossary

PQC
Post-quantum cryptography: encryption and signatures designed to stay secure against future quantum computers.
ML-KEM
The NIST post-quantum standard (FIPS 203) for agreeing a secret key over the network.
ML-DSA
The NIST post-quantum standard (FIPS 204) for digital signatures.
Nonce
A random number used once; it makes every challenge unique so old evidence cannot be reused.
Transaction hash
A fingerprint of the payment details; the evidence is signed together with it, so it cannot approve a different payment.
Replay
Sending evidence that was already used once, hoping it is accepted again.
Presentation attack
Showing the camera a photo, a screen or a mask instead of a live face.
Morph
One face photo blended from two people, so that both could pass as its owner.
Deepfake
A face generated or swapped by AI.
Injection
Feeding a fake video stream into the app through a virtual camera instead of a real one.
APCER
Share of attacks wrongly accepted (lower is better).
BPCER
Share of genuine users wrongly rejected (lower is better).
EER
The error rate at the setting where APCER equals BPCER.
AUC
How well a detector separates attacks from genuine samples: 1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is guessing.
